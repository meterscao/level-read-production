# Cover Direction

Create one 5:3 horizontal, text-free Level Read editorial cover. It should make a world-news story feel clear, contemporary, approachable, and worth opening. Level Read uses photographic reality and bold editorial geometry together: the photograph gives the news credibility; the graphic structure makes its central relationship visible.

## Core visual system

- Keep the image simple. Use one substantial primary photographic subject—or, when the news is inherently about several actors, one clearly related primary group—and only the supporting context needed to make one news relationship clear. Do not try to illustrate every fact in the article or choose one arbitrary hero from a genuinely multi-subject story.
- Use a bright, high-key pale-colour canvas: very light warm grey, mist blue, pale sage, pale blush, soft sand-peach, or quiet grey-lilac. Choose a tint that lightly answers a meaningful subject colour, but keep it airy and close to warm white.
- Give the canvas an almost imperceptible, low-contrast contemporary matte variation so it does not feel digitally sterile. At normal viewing size it must read as a clean, smooth pure colour—not as speckles, paper fibre, dirt, mottling, or texture. It must never look yellowed, archival, distressed, fibrous, or like old paper.
- Preserve the natural colour, material, and distinctive details of the main real subject when they matter. Use grey-white or low-saturation treatment for secondary photographic elements when it helps the natural subject and vector colour read clearly. Do not default either to all-monochrome or to full-colour everywhere.
- Limit the colour budget: one strong, high-chroma vector colour; at most one smaller supporting vector colour; the pale canvas; and only the natural colours necessary to the subject. This restraint makes the image lively rather than busy.
- Build the cover in three clear layers: (1) a photographic subject or factual scene fragment that carries the news; (2) only the minimum real environment, naturally dissolving into the pale canvas rather than filling the frame; and (3) one vector structure that gives the composition its editorial character. A full, specific background is rarely necessary.
- Scale a vector structure by its narrative role, not by a fixed small footprint. It is secondary in **semantic hierarchy**, not necessarily visually timid: one bold, opaque colour structure may occupy roughly 15–30% of the frame when it makes the story relationship immediately legible. It must stay visually simpler and less detailed than the photographic subject, sit behind, beside, or structurally beneath it rather than replace its action, and preserve clear space around the subject's face and decisive gesture.
- Use clean edges, generous negative space, and a crisp photographic montage. Avoid unaltered full-frame stock-photo compositions, invitation stationery, generic advertising imagery, and decorative visual clutter.

## Story-first vector structure

Before generating, identify the one visible news fact, tension, or contrast that should read at a glance; the cover need not explain the complete article. Then state one active **story verb** and choose one vector structure that makes that reading visible. The photographic subject or group must receive the first visual attention; the vector structure is a confident editorial intervention that clarifies its relationship. It may have a distinctive silhouette and substantial presence, but must never become a second subject, a complete abstract poster, or a reusable backdrop.

- **Travels, flows, connects, avoids, arrives:** use a single route, curve, channel, or connected band. It must resolve naturally into the photographic world; never turn it into a map, literal arrow, or information graphic.
- **Expands, increases, extends, changes, upgrades:** use stepped blocks, offset planes, a lengthened field, or a decisive geometric extension. Do not turn policy news into a calendar, a ribbon, or a generic celebratory graphic.
- **Crosses, contains, breaks through, protects:** use a gate, boundary, cut, enclosure, opening, or clear spatial divide.
- **Reveals, distinguishes, separates, withholds:** use a cut plane, interrupted platform, framed opening, stepped edge, or a deliberate gap.
- **Balances, compares, shares, spans generations:** use controlled parallel forms, a support, a bridge, a divided field, or a measured asymmetry.
- **Competes, overtakes, predicts, negotiates space:** use a short chicane, compressed offset lanes, or a small spatial cut that shows interaction among the group. Do not turn the cover into a full racetrack, map, HUD, or information graphic.

Choose geometry with a clear visual posture from the story rather than repeating the most recent cover. Broken platforms, stepped cut planes, offset fields, folded planes, framed openings, field edges, controlled arches, curves, bands, blocks, and small accents can all belong to the Level Read family. The vector must meet the subject's ground, edge, movement, or real context; it should feel structurally integrated rather than pasted onto a photograph. A large circle is permitted only when it is the clearest factual or conceptual form—for example a planet, ball, sun, cycle, balance, or time—and never as the default shape behind a subject.

Use one primary vector structure and, only if it adds rhythm or balance, one compact secondary accent. Do not default to a thin line, a small square, or a generic backdrop when a more particular silhouette would make the cover memorable. A substantial block is appropriate when it clarifies the story verb at a glance. In every case, it must meet the photographic field rather than float as an independent design, remain simpler than the subject, and leave the subject's face and central action unobstructed. Preserve ample pale-canvas breathing room. Keep shapes opaque, flat, matte, and pure-coloured: no gradients, translucent overlays, curled ribbons, decorative waves, radiating rays, flags, or arbitrary stickers.

## Photographic subject and reference policy

When the user supplies or explicitly clears a reference image of a person, animal, place, object, or event, use it as the subject anchor. Preserve recognisable anatomy, markings, identity, material reality, and meaningful details. For a group reference, preserve the essential count, interaction, and distinctive visual cues of the group when they are central to the news. Retain the decisive subject or scene evidence, but crop away incidental setting and let it dissolve into the pale canvas when that produces a clearer cover. Build the crop, negative space, and vector relationship around the reference; do not replace it with a generic generated version.

Without a supplied reference, do not make an identifiable, polished lifestyle protagonist. When an ordinary person is contextually necessary, make them non-identifying and observational: distant, side-on, from behind, partly occluded, or a small part of a real setting. Prefer a precise place, object, route, tool, animal, or environmental detail whenever it can tell the story more honestly.

## Tone and sensitive subjects

- Discovery, science, animals, culture, learning, and human-interest stories can feel bright, curious, and welcoming.
- International, business, technology, and policy stories should feel calm, clear, and intelligent—not bureaucratic or visually inert.
- Conflict, disaster, death, historical trauma, and political tension need restraint. Prefer a grounded scene, object, or quiet relationship; do not make suffering decorative or sensational.

Avoid political-campaign, wartime-poster, archival-propaganda, or national-design-stereotype energy. In particular, avoid heroic low angles, rally-like crowds, black-and-white figures on dominant red fields, radiating lines, and aggressive poster diagonals.

Do not use lettering, headlines, fake text, numbers, logos, watermarks, UI, or generic symbols unless a verified, user-supplied reference makes a particular real-world mark indispensable. Do not use glossy 3D renders, arbitrary maps, or generic hands unless human agency is central and the hands are reference-led.

## Cover plan and acceptance gate

Before prompting the image model, state: the one visible news fact, tension, or contrast to make legible; the subject-reference decision; whether the primary subject is an individual, group, or factual scene fragment; the story verb; the three-layer composition (photographic subject, minimal real context, pale canvas); the single vector structure and its visual posture; its secondary semantic role; the limited palette; the named pale canvas tone; the supporting context; and the exclusions most likely to prevent a visual cliché for this subject.

Inspect at full size and thumbnail size. Regenerate rather than finalize if any answer is no:

- Is there one clear primary subject or group, with only the context needed to understand it? For a group story, are all essential members and their relationship visible without inventing a hero?
- Does the cover make one visible news fact, contrast, or tension legible at a glance, without trying to explain every cause and detail?
- Does the background retain only the minimum factual environment, then cleanly dissolve into an airy pale canvas rather than becoming a full scene?
- Does the vector structure make the story verb visible while remaining secondary to the photographic subject or group, rather than becoming a second subject or an abstract poster? It may be bold and distinctively shaped; a substantial colour block can still be secondary if the subject receives attention first at thumbnail size.
- Has the vector been given a story-specific silhouette and structural connection to the photographic field, rather than defaulting to a timid line, a generic block, or decorative filler?
- Is the palette limited, with one dominant vector colour and readable grey-white/natural-colour contrast?
- Is the canvas light, subtly textured, and contemporary rather than pure sterile white, dark grey, or yellowed paper?
- Does this cover avoid the recently repeated circle, palette, composition, and geometry combination?
- If a reference subject was supplied, are its recognisable features retained?
- Does the image avoid topic-specific clichés—for example maps and arrows for migration, or invitation/ribbon aesthetics for marriage policy?
- Has every explanatory element earned its place, or would removing one make the direct reading clearer?

## Canonical cover master

Deliver one canonical master at **1500 × 900 px**, a fixed 5:3 horizontal ratio. Compose and crop to 5:3; never stretch an image to fit. Export the approved master as an sRGB PNG with no post-generation Level Read logo, signature, or watermark.
